Across Borders, Unequal Futures.

SIB × YSP International Youth Essay Competition 2026

The systems that connect our world do not affect everyone equally. From the workers behind artificial intelligence to the communities supplying the green transition, whose experiences remain outside the frame? We invite young writers to examine one international issue closely, challenge familiar assumptions and make a compelling case for change.

01 / About

Look Closer. Connect The Dots.

International debates are often told through governments, institutions and headline numbers. This competition asks you to look at the people, choices and unequal relationships within them.

Social Issues Bubble and Youth for Sustainable Policy invite essays exploring how social justice and sustainability intersect across borders.

Choose one prompt. Investigate a specific case. Consider who benefits, who bears the costs and what a fairer response could involve.

You do not need to cover an entire region or solve a global problem. A focused argument, supported by credible evidence and attentive to local context, can reveal more than a sweeping overview.

02 / The Prompts

Six Questions. Your Perspective.

Choose one of the following prompts. You may focus on one country, community, agreement, project or supply chain. Compare cases where doing so strengthens your argument.

  1. Prompt01

    The care-worker migration bargain

    When countries recruit nurses and care workers internationally, who should bear responsibility for the resulting care gaps in their countries of origin?

    Examine a specific migration corridor, recruitment arrangement or policy. Consider workers’ freedom to move alongside the needs of families, communities and health systems.

  2. Prompt02

    The hidden price of the green transition

    Can the global energy transition be just when critical-mineral extraction proceeds without meaningful Indigenous consent?

    Examine one project or supply chain. Consider who defines consent, how benefits and harms are distributed, and what accountability should involve.

  3. Prompt03

    Moving without losing home

    How should migration agreements protect the political agency, cultural continuity and rights of Pacific communities facing climate displacement?

    Focus on a particular community or agreement. Consider how mobility can expand choices while protecting the ability to remain, cultural connections and self-determination.

  4. Prompt04

    After the shipping container

    When used clothing, plastics or electronic waste crosses borders, where should the exporting country’s responsibility end?

    Trace one material or trade route. Distinguish reuse and repair from waste disposal, and consider the livelihoods and environmental burdens involved.

  5. Prompt05

    The people behind the machine

    What obligations should AI companies have toward outsourced data annotators and content moderators in lower-income countries?

    Examine a defined part of the AI labour supply chain. Consider working conditions, bargaining power, psychological wellbeing and responsibility across subcontracting arrangements.

  6. Prompt06

    A passport to participation

    How do visa rules, travel costs and funding arrangements shape whose voices influence international climate negotiations?

    Examine a particular negotiation, delegation or participation mechanism. Consider the difference between being present, being heard and having influence.

These questions invite critical examination, not a predetermined answer. You may challenge a prompt’s assumptions if you explain your reasoning and support your position with evidence.

03 / What We Look For

Depth Over Breadth.

We are looking for a clear argument that helps readers understand an issue more fully.

01

A focused case

Define the people, place, policy or relationship you are examining. Explain why this case matters.

02

Evidence with context

Use credible sources and explain what they establish. Distinguish documented findings from interpretation and uncertainty.

03

A serious counterargument

Engage with a competing perspective fairly. Show where your argument remains persuasive and where its limits lie.

04

A considered response

Explain what should change, who could act and what trade-offs or implementation barriers they would face.

04 / Eligibility

Who Can Enter?

  • 01Open to individuals aged 16–30 on 1 December 2026.
  • 02Open internationally, subject to applicable legal restrictions.
  • 03Entry is free.
  • 04One individual entry per person.
  • 05Essays must be written in English.
  • 06Length: 1,000–1,500 words, excluding the title, citations and reference list. Substantive footnotes count toward the limit.
  • 07Submit original work that has not previously been published.
  • 08Remove your name and other identifying details from the essay file.
  • 09Accepted file formats: PDF or DOCX.

Fair participation

We welcome perspectives from different countries and educational backgrounds. Essays will be assessed on the quality of their argument and evidence, rather than nationality, institutional prestige or elaborate vocabulary.

Using AI tools

Basic spelling and grammar assistance is permitted. You must develop your own argument, analysis and prose. AI-generated essays, paragraphs, translations and fabricated references are not permitted. Disclose any permitted assistance in the submission form. Integrity concerns receive human review; automated detector scores alone do not determine disqualification.

05 / Judging

How Essays Will Be Assessed.

  1. Argument and critical analysis

    30%

    A coherent position, sustained reasoning and meaningful engagement with counterarguments.

  2. Evidence and source use

    25%

    Relevant, credible evidence used accurately and with attention to context and limitations.

  3. Originality and insight

    20%

    Independent thinking, perceptive connections and a distinctive approach to the chosen case.

  4. Practical implications

    15%

    A thoughtful assessment of possible responses, responsibilities and trade-offs.

  5. Clarity and structure

    10%

    Readable writing, logical organisation and precise communication.

09 / FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions.

Full rules ↗Privacy notice ↗Enquiries ↗
Do I need previous essay competition experience?

No. We welcome first-time entrants. Your essay should demonstrate careful thinking, credible research and a clear argument.

Can I write about my own country or community?

Yes. A locally grounded case can reveal important international relationships. Make the connection to your selected prompt explicit.

Do I have to compare two countries?

No. Comparison is optional. A focused single case is welcome.

Can I disagree with the question?

Yes. You may question its assumptions if you explain your position and support it with evidence.

Does English have to be my first language?

No. Clear reasoning and communication matter more than elaborate language.

Can I submit with a co-author?

No. Each person may submit one individual entry.

What counts toward the word limit?

The essay body, including headings and substantive footnotes, counts. The title, citations and reference list do not.

Can I use AI tools?

Basic spelling and grammar assistance is permitted. You must develop your own argument, analysis and prose. AI-generated essays, paragraphs, translations and fabricated references are not permitted. Disclose any permitted assistance in the submission form. Integrity concerns receive human review; automated detector scores alone do not determine disqualification.

Is there a cash prize?

No cash prize is offered at present. Recognised entrants receive digital award certificates.

Will my essay automatically be published?

No. Selected essays may be considered for publication, and only with the author’s agreement.

Do I keep copyright?

Yes. Any publication would be agreed separately with the author.

How will my personal information be used?

Entrant information is used to administer the competition, as set out in the competition privacy notice.
