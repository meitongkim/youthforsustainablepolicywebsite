    From Policy to Action: Malaysian Youth Driving Sustainability.

    By Afiq Danial bin Nasir · Ambassador · 20 · Malaysia

    “In reality, it is also a governance issue, an education issue, and a participation issue.”

    Malaysia’s sustainability agenda is often considered as if it were prominently an environmental issue. In reality, it is also a governance issue, an education issue, and a participation issue. Malaysia does not simply require more awareness in regards to sustainable development, but a stronger public sense of responsibility about sustainability, with emphasis on the young people who will be the ones living in the future with the repercussions of today’s sustainable policy position.

    First and foremost, Important roles in Malaysia's sustainable landscape are already occupied by the youth. They do not belong to the marginal group waiting to be referred to at the final part of the process, but a leading social electorate with the great potential to influence public priorities, social etiquette and long-term development of the culture. According to Ahmad et al. (2025), he argued that the youth stance deserve proper recognition as young people make up almost half of the population and are stationed to carry out the leadership roles in environmental sustainability. If the youth population of Malaysia are not meaningfully engaged in this important effort, sustainability remains as something proclaimed from an isolated decision makers rather than practiced across society.

    Subsequently, there are suggestions that highlight the engagement of the Malaysian youth in the sustainability agenda with the emphasis on their uneven involvement in the effort. University students seem to possess a marginal comprehension of the Sustainable development Goals (SDG), with educational establishments and social media platforms performing as their primary sources of exposure (Ismail et al., 2022). Nevertheless, awareness alone does not guarantee depth of insight, also environment - related SDG’s still require greater attention than they currently receive.

    Youth-led SDG programmes in Malaysia found that youth movements do engage national and global SDG collaborators, yet their engagement remains limited (Yusuf & Harsono, 2022). More precisely, youth participation is present, however it is not yet fully incorporated into the wider system of decision-making and implementation.

    Furthermore, volunteerism offers one practical and reliable bridge between youth energy and policy delivery. Malaysian work in SDG-based volunteerism describes volunteering as a channel that unites people into planning and decision-making processes while establishing interaction between government and the public (Thomas & Ghazali, 2024). Simultaneously, youth engagement cannot be prolonged by structure alone as communication matters. This is due to the barriers established by limited access to policy information, as well as weak perceived agency. Clearer communication, mainstream youth spaces such as social media needed to be regarded as a better model of communication, with a shift from top to down authority to capacity-sharing that regards young people as partners rather than passive audiences.

    Therefore, Malaysia’s sustainable future will depend not only on the existing policies, but also on who is trusted to carry them forward to a better outcome. An extremely reliable national approach should be the gamechanger in viewing youth as the hope of the nation in terms of sustainable future rather than viewing them as campaign audiences. Youth should be treated as important figures in governance and to the everyday culture that sustainable requires. The change would definitely not solve every policy gap, but it will definitely bring Malaysia closer to a sustainability model that is durable and better grounded in the society it is trying to transform.

    • Thomas & Ghazali (2024) — Youth participation in SDG-oriented volunteerism ↗

This framework is subject to review and more recommendation from policy experts and other technocrats and will require a lot of effort by various stakeholders for it to become a tool that will leaders be accountable.

    What Is the UK Climate Change Act and Why Does It Matter?

    By Aiyesha Kaur Swarnn · Ambassador · 25 · United Kingdom

    “Climate targets set the destination, but meaningful action determines whether we get there.”

    The Climate Change Act 2008 is one of the UK's most important pieces of climate legislation. But what does it actually do?

    Put simply, the Act created a legal framework for the UK to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and prepare for the impacts of climate change. It originally committed the UK to reducing greenhouse gas emissions by at least 80% by 2050. In 2019, this was strengthened to a net-zero emissions target by 2050.

    One of the Act's most important features is the system of carbon budgets. Rather than only setting a long-term target, the UK sets limits on the amount of greenhouse gases that can be emitted over successive five-year periods. This creates a pathway towards the 2050 target and allows progress to be assessed along the way.

    For example, if the UK wants to reach net zero by 2050, it cannot wait until 2049 to begin reducing emissions. Carbon budgets encourage action now across sectors such as energy, transport, buildings and industry.

    The Act also established the Climate Change Committee (CCC), an independent body that advises the government on emissions targets and monitors progress. This provides an important mechanism for assessing whether government policies are consistent with the UK's climate commitments.

    Why does this matter to young people? Climate policy is ultimately about shaping the conditions in which future generations will live. Decisions about energy, transport, housing and infrastructure made today will influence both the UK's emissions and the opportunities available to young people tomorrow.

    The Climate Change Act demonstrates how legislation can turn climate ambitions into long-term policy commitments. However, having a legal target is only the beginning. The challenge is ensuring that government action, investment and everyday policies are strong enough to meet it.

    Climate targets set the destination, but meaningful action determines whether we get there.

    The Melting Water Towers: Glacial Volatility and the Crisis of Governance in the Nepal Himalayas.

    By Ankur Koirala · Ambassador · 28 · Nepal

    “We are the first responders to a crisis we did not cause, using resources we did not have.”

    From a young age, we in the mountain region are told that the Himalayas are the eternal “Water Towers of Asia”, a permanent source of life for the billions downstream. We are taught to take pride in their majesty. But as the climate crisis accelerates, that pride is being replaced by a profound sense of precarity. In Nepal, and specifically in the Rasuwa District, climate change is no longer a distant academic threat or a challenge for the future generations to navigate. That distance has vanished.

    As glaciers in the Langtang and Ganesh Himal ranges undergo unprecedented thinning and the Trishuli River basin becomes increasingly volatile, we are forced to confront a blunt reality: a warming Himalaya is no longer just an ecological point of observation. It is a systemic crisis of governance, energy, and security, and with the recent destructive flooding – also a crisis of survival.

    The instability unfolding in our high-altitude landscapes is driven by a phenomenon known as “Elevation Dependent Warming” (EDW). While global climate summits focus on the 1.5°C average, the reality is that high-altitude terrains like Rasuwa are warming at a significantly faster rate than the global mean. This is not a theoretical curiosity for the scientific community; it is an existential threat to the lifeblood of Nepal’s economy. The Trishuli River corridor – a vital artery for national hydropower and trans-border state – now sits directly in the path of the potential Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs) and catastrophic debris flows. When these glaciers retreat, they leave behind unstable proglacial lakes and self-formed embankments, essentially ticking bombs held back by the fragile natural dams of ice and loose rock.

    However, to actually grasp the crisis in Rasuwa, we must look at what lies beneath the surface of these “natural” disasters. Much like the concept of the “personal carbon footprint” that was famously popularized by British Petroleum to deflect systemic blame from the oil industry onto individuals, the discourse around Himalayan disasters often masquerades a deeper climate injustice. Nepal contributes less than 0.02% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Yet, we are the ones forced to divert our limited national budget towards mitigating disasters, fueled by the 80% of emissions produced by just 57 global fossil fuel and cement producers. In the high valleys of Rasuwa, we are effectively paying environmental and economic debt we did not accrue. We are the first responders to a crisis we did not cause, using resources we did not have.

    The tragedy is compounded by a profound “static data” problem in our governance. A significant portion of Nepal’s national infrastructure, our bridges, roads and billion-dollar hydropower projects, was engineered based on the historical hydrological data that is now fundamentally obsolete. We are witnessing a dangerous “engineering lag” where the infrastructure being built today is designed for a climate that no longer exists. For example, the Upper Trishuli-1 and other cascade projects are the backbone of Nepal’s energy future, yet they are vulnerable to sediment loads and flood volumes that were previously considered “thousand-year events" but are now becoming decadal realities. When a flood wipes out a trekking trail or a landslide severs a trade route, it is often labeled as an “act of God". In reality, it is often an act of administrative negligence – the result of a policy gap where building codes have failed to catch up with the velocity of the melting ice.

    Furthermore, we must address the “Embedded Risk" in our development models. Just as the American automotive industry lobbied to make cars a necessity, Nepal’s development has become heavily reliant on “corridor-based growth”. Our trade and energy are connected in narrow, high-risk valleys. When the Trishuli corridor is blocked by a climate-induced landslide, the entire nation feels the economic tremor. This concentration of risk is a governance choice. We have prioritized rapid infrastructure expansion over resilient, decentralized planning. The result is a system where a single glacial outburst at the source can trigger a national crisis and trade blockade.

    Nepal’s policy response, while ambiguous on paper, reveals a persistent disconnect between the administrative center in Kathmandu and the frontline communities in the mountains. Our second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) claims for all 753 governments to implement the Local Adaptation Plans of Action (LAPA) by 2035. However, we must be clear: “paper adaptation” does not stop a GLOF. The challenge lies in turning these bureaucratic commitments into functioning, localized systems that are adequately funded and technically supported. Adaptation cannot remain a reactive measure initiated only after a valley has been devastated. It must be woven into the very being of land-use planning, disaster risk mapping, and automated early-warning systems, long before a crisis hits. This requires a radical shift from “disaster relief”, which is essentially just the management of tragedy, to “proactive governance”.

    Crucially, the governance revolution requires a fundamental re-evaluation of the role of young people. We are often patronized as the “leaders of tomorrow", but in the context of the climate crisis in Rasuwa, we are the risk managers of today. Youth in these districts are not just passive victims of shifting landscapes; they are the primary observers. They are the ones using digital tools to track the impact and changes, trace-and-rescue, lead local relief efforts and question the safety of the infrastructure being built in their backyards. Our participation should not be limited to awareness campaigns or tokenistic youth councils. We need a meaningful seat at the decision-making table, with the power to audit infrastructural resilience and oversee the transparent use of climate finance.

    The problem we face in the Himalayas is a microcosm of a global failure in accountability. The idea that we are “all in this together” is only true in the sense that the whole world goes down if even one part of it works against the others. If the “Water Towers of Asia” fail due to a combination of global corporate negligence and local administrative inertia, the impact will cascade down to over a billion people in the South Asian plains.

    Nepal’s climate challenge is no longer about recognizing that the mountains are melting. It is about building institutions that are as dynamic as the landscape they serve. We must ensure our policies move as quickly as our retreating glaciers, for the stability of our basins and the safety of our downstream communities depend entirely on the courage and scientific integrity of our decisions today.

    Decent Work Isn't Just About Jobs: What Malawi's Young People Need from Sustainable Development Policy.

    By Mike Store · Ambassador · 22 · Malawi

    “What kind of work are we creating, and does it build dignified and meaningful lives for young people?”

    The transition into adulthood can be an exciting time for a lot of Malawian young men and women, bringing with it prospects of social and economic independence by having decent jobs. While securing any form of income can feel like a success for a lot of the youths, employment alone does not necessarily equate to decent work.

    The ILO Director-General, Gilbert Houngbo said “Creating decent jobs for young people is not just a social imperative, but one of the smartest investments that a country can make” (ILO, 2026). When researchers, policy experts and groups in the academia converse on youth unemployment in Malawi, I have observed that such conversations often involve one generic question; “How many jobs can we create?” However, I feel the need of going deeper with the conversation by asking that “What kind of work are we creating, and does it build dignified and meaningful lives for young people?”

    I conducted an independent research project titled Challenges to Gender Equality in the Malawi Decent Work Country Programme. During this study, I examined over 50 national and global labour policies and regulatory frameworks, engaged with the Malawi Tripartite Labour Advisory Council to verify labour administration, and also 30 female workers, whose significant portion were youthful. From this experience alone, I became exposed to how decisions made at policy level directly affects working conditions, rights and opportunities for the youth. Consequently, I understood that good policies fail to translate into meaningful outcomes if the intended youthful populations they are meant to serve are neither heard nor consulted. Malawian youth also need fair working conditions, social protection, enjoyment of working rights and equally participate in decision making processes in terms of policy making.

    I understand meaningful youth participation as something that goes far beyond creating space for the youth to be present; is an intentional process of recognizing, nurturing and valuing young people’s vital contributions as agents of positive change by giving them opportunities to support decision making and offer practical solutions to numerous challenges facing them.

    The Malawian youth should not merely be viewed as beneficiaries waiting for jobs, but also as entrepreneurs and innovators capable of shaping Malawi’s development aspirations and defining their own destiny. Youth development policies in Malawi must go beyond quantity of jobs, but also the quality of the jobs to provide opportunity, dignity and a sustainable future worth building for all.

    The Future Is Sitting in Our Classrooms.

    By Tuyizere Schadrack · Ambassador · 19 · Rwanda

    “The future is not far away. It is sitting in our classrooms today.”

    When we talk about the future, we often imagine something far away—a better economy, stronger communities, new technologies, and a more peaceful world.

    But perhaps we are looking in the wrong direction.

    The future is not far away. It is sitting in our classrooms today.

    It is the student raising a hand with a question. It is the young person learning a new skill on a phone. It is the student who dreams of becoming a doctor, teacher, engineer, entrepreneur, scientist, or community leader. It is the young person who sees a problem in their community and wonders, “What can I do about it?”

    Young people are not simply waiting for their future to arrive. Many are already trying to build it.

    Yet potential needs opportunity.

    A young person may have determination but lack learning materials. They may have a brilliant idea but lack internet access. They may have knowledge but lack mentorship. They may have ambition but face financial barriers that make their dreams seem distant.

    This is why education must be understood as more than examinations and certificates.

    Education should give young people the ability to understand problems, question existing solutions, create new ideas, and take meaningful action.

    In today's world, digital technology can make learning more accessible than ever. A smartphone can become a classroom, a library, a communication tool, and a window to opportunities around the world. But access to technology alone is not enough. Young people also need guidance to use it responsibly and productively.

    We should therefore ask ourselves an important question:

    What would happen if every young person had a genuine opportunity to develop their potential?

    Perhaps a student interested in science could develop an idea that improves healthcare in their community. Perhaps a young person passionate about agriculture could discover a better way of supporting farmers. Perhaps someone who loves teaching could help another student discover their own ability.

    The impact of one opportunity can travel much further than we imagine.

    This is why investing in young people is not charity. It is an investment in the future of communities and the world.

    Schools, governments, families, organizations, and communities all have a role to play. Young people need quality education, mentorship, digital skills, leadership opportunities, and spaces where their voices are taken seriously.

    At the same time, young people must also recognize their own responsibility. We cannot wait for someone else to build the future for us. We must keep learning, asking questions, supporting one another, and using whatever opportunities we have to create positive change.

    A classroom may look like an ordinary room, but inside it are people who could one day solve extraordinary problems.

    The student learning today could become tomorrow's scientist.

    The student practicing communication could become tomorrow's leader.

    The student helping a classmate could become tomorrow's community builder.

    And the young person who refuses to give up despite obstacles could inspire an entire generation.

    So perhaps the question is not, “What will young people do for the future?”

    Perhaps the better question is:

    “What can we do today to give young people the opportunity to shape it?”

    Because the future is already learning.

    The future is already dreaming.

    And the future is sitting in our classrooms.

    What we choose to give those young people today will help determine the world we all live in tomorrow.

    From Climate Awareness to Climate Action: The Role of Rwanda’s Youth.

    By Ndatimana Fabrice · Ambassador · 19 · Rwanda

    “Climate action should not be something young people only learn about. It should be something they are empowered to help shape.”

    Rwanda is often recognized for its efforts to protect the environment and promote sustainable development. Yet for young people, the challenge is not only understanding climate change—it is turning that understanding into meaningful action.

    Young people represent a large part of Rwanda’s population and have the creativity, energy, and ability to influence their communities. Climate action does not always require large projects. It can begin with simple community initiatives: protecting trees, reducing waste, promoting clean environments, educating other young people, and developing innovative solutions to local environmental challenges.

    However, youth participation should go beyond volunteering. Young people should also have opportunities to contribute their ideas to discussions about environmental policies and sustainable development. Schools, youth organizations, local communities, and policymakers can create spaces where young people learn how policies are made and can share perspectives from their communities.

    Technology can also help. Rwanda’s young innovators can use digital platforms to raise awareness, organize environmental campaigns, share practical knowledge, and develop solutions suited to local needs. Connecting these initiatives with policy can make youth contributions even more effective.

    The transition from climate awareness to climate action requires young people to see themselves not only as people affected by environmental challenges, but also as contributors to solving them. Rwanda’s progress toward sustainable development will be stronger when its youth are informed, included, and given opportunities to act.

    Climate action should not be something young people only learn about. It should be something they are empowered to help shape.

    The Myth of the Personal Carbon Footprint.

    By Shubhanyu Chandra · Ambassador · 15 · New Zealand

    “Consumerism was spoonfed to humans for generations by companies who make billions of dollars off it.”

    From a young age we are told that it is our responsibility to fight against climate change. Why? For two reasons: because it is the planet we inhabit to which there is no Planet B and because we are responsible for the state our planet has reached. Climate change could have been less disastrous if only we the people had done this or not. That is partially true. The problem arises when we are told that we are equally responsible for what's happened to our world. That all we need to do is EVs or switch to eco-friendly straws. For if you average how much individuals across the world emit in CO2 each year, it comes to around 4.5 tonnes. There are companies which produce this much in a matter of minutes. So how did the blame get passed onto us and the small actions we take? This misplacing of no blame is no accident and part of it can be attributed to the very entities who most benefit from it.

    Let’s be clear, there are actions all of us take which we ought to cut back on. We as humans engage in this sort of consumerism where overconsumption has become the norm. What makes it easier is that most of us don’t see the waste we collate and the impact it has. But the 2.6 billion tonnes of solid waste we produce each year has to go somewhere. It certainly isn’t going off to outer space. All of us would do good to cut back on that alongside the dozens of tiny habits we should adopt. All of this is our reality. But there are two things we ought to remember alongside that:

    The first is that for all the harm we do to the planet, it's still very minimal to what corporations are up to. Companies like Nestle and Coco Cola generate millions of tonnes in waste each year through their plastic companies. And that’s just one instance of the way they harm our environment. Out of all the greenhouse gas emissions produced each year, 80% of them can be attributed to 37 fossil fuel and cement producers. Companies which emit more than many countries do. And they don’t show any signs of stopping. Post-Paris agreement, 55% of privately-owned fossil fuel companies actually raised their emissions. That is just going off the information which has been provided to us. These publicly traded companies have a habit of underreporting their carbon emissions, with 58% of them engaging in misreporting. What are the emissions of your average human against that?

    The second point is that many of the non-climate-friendly actions we commit are done because they are so embedded into our societies. Why are they so embedded into our society? Because companies made sure they were. The most egregious example of that can be found with the American Automotive Industry. They aggressively lobbied the government on every matter they would. Cars were reclassified from ‘luxury good’ to ‘vital economic tool’ which came with all sorts of tax breaks. A tax which was supposed to go towards public transit was redirected to road construction and highways were prioritized time and time again over car alternatives. More and more space was allocated for parking space, thus destroying the idea of the walkable city which made cars even more necessary. Now cars are one of the biggest ways humans produce emissions. All because the industry made it a necessity.

    But we see every industry imaginable. The agriculture industry which got the government to heavily subsidise meat production so that people would spend more money on it. The fashion industry who launched successful marketing campaigns for all sorts of products. Technology companies who stop updating their products for a few years so that it’s necessary to get new devices, thus contributing to e-waste. Consumerism didn’t occur because it was the natural human instinct. Consumerism was spoonfed to humans for generations by companies who make billions of dollars off it. Even as they make us more wasteful beings along the way.

    So how did the blame get passed onto us ordinary people? The idea of the ‘personal carbon footprint’ first graced the human lexicon in 2004. Who spread it? British Petroleum, the same company that emits 92,300 tonnes of CO2 daily. They launched a public relationships campaign on how each human should be tracking their personal carbon footprint. They were even kind enough to produce a carbon footprint calculator. Was this out of the goodness of their heart? As lovely as it would be to imagine that these companies did so out of care, it’s important to remember these were the same companies that spent millions of dollars on climate denial think tanks. All while ExxonMobil’s scientists estimated their contribution to global warming back in the 1970s. So why do they tell us to care about our carbon footprint? Because it benefits them in two ways.

    The first part is that it alienates a large number of people from the climate effort. Most people aren’t too fond of being told what to do. Especially when it comes to changing their habits. And there are quite a lot of actions the climate-friendly camp encourages–like adopting plastic straws or going vegan-which don’t quite make sense as to what impact they make to better the planet. Now people can see the state of our world. They know that action needs to be taken. They just don’t know how giving up on the better parts of their life aligns with that. It can end up looking like a whole lot of virtue signalling that ends up alienating a significant number of people. And so would-be climate activists are lost.

    The second reason the idea of the personal carbon footprint is beneficial is because of how easily it deflects blame from companies. Everybody is too focused, feeling guilty for their actions or being annoyed that climate change is being pinned on them. These people don’t have the resources to go up against fossil fuel companies. They aren’t even educated on how negatively impactful these companies are. Until now. There is a greater understanding of intersectionality as people are beginning to realise how much companies have contributed to climate change. This can scare companies which is why we’re seeing attempts to label these activists as radicals. But one can hope people are aware enough to not fall victim to that. They certainly deserve better.

    This isn’t to say we shouldn’t appreciate people who give up on something dear to reduce their own carbon emissions. They might only have a small impact but it's an impact nonetheless. The problem is that such a stand is more about personal principles than it is about actually making an impact on climate change. For that we need to mitigate its worst offenders. The people who are responsible for 80% of the world’s emissions. Because the whole world is going down if even one part of it works against all the others.
