Who Receives the Dividend? Angola’s Fuel Subsidy Reform Needs an Automatic Social Guarantee

Kaashvi SheoranUniversity of Amsterdam · Environmental economics · Netherlands
SummarySince 2023, Angola has phased out fuel subsidies that once functioned as the country's only substantial social protection, following the collapse of health, education, and welfare spending after 2015. The reform has delivered real fiscal savings and a modelled climate benefit, but compensation through the Kwenda cash transfer programme has reached only a fraction of exposed households. In July 2025, a diesel price increase triggered nationwide unrest that left 22 people dead, forcing the government to postpone full subsidy removal to 2028. This brief argues the reform is fiscally effective but socially fragile, and that Angola's debt service obligations create a real risk that savings are absorbed before compensation is funded. It recommends a legislated automatic compensation trigger, capping the share of savings diverted to debt service and guaranteeing verified household payment before any future price increase takes effect.

When governments remove fossil fuel subsidies, the standard debate asks whether poorer households were compensated. Angola’s phased reform, launched on 1 June 2023, poses a harder question: What happens when the subsidy being removed has functioned as the country’s broadest welfare instrument? The first adjustment raised petrol prices from Kz160 to Kz300 per litre, an 87.5% increase that cut petrol subsidies by an estimated 45% (International Monetary Fund [IMF], 2024). Diesel adjustments followed in April 2024 and twice in 2025, culminating in a price of Kz400 per litre in July (Bambe et al., 2024; Government of Angola, 2025).

The reform’s significance lies in what preceded it. After oil revenues fell sharply in 2015, fiscal consolidation reduced spending on health, education, social protection, and water, leaving fuel subsidies as the only de facto social spending. Subsidy expenditure subsequently climbed from 1.5% of GDP in 2019 to 3.7% in 2023 (Bambe et al., 2024). Angola was not merely subsidising fuel. It was routing its social contract through the petrol pump. The reform must therefore be judged against two tests: fiscal repair and welfare replacement.

Fiscally Effective, Socially Fragile

On the fiscal test, early results are meaningful. The IMF estimated that diesel subsidy reductions in the first half of 2025 saved approximately 0.3% of GDP, while projecting that total fuel subsidy expenditure would fall from 2.7% of GDP in 2024 to 1.3% in 2025 (IMF, 2025). The reform also carries a modelled climate benefit of 43.7 MtCO2e in avoided emissions between 2024 and 2035 (World Bank, 2024a). Its initial sequencing was economically sound because petrol subsidies were especially regressive and their removal had comparatively limited direct effects on poorer households (Bambe et al., 2024).

The welfare test exposes the design flaw. Compensation relies principally on the Kwenda cash transfer programme, which pays approximately US$12 per month to about one million poor households, an amount modelled as sufficient to cover the reform’s average welfare effects (Climate Investment Funds [CIF], n.d.). Adequacy, however, is not the same as reach. Approximately 3.2 million urban residents living in poverty remain largely outside social safety nets, while 6.3 million poor rural residents face enrolment barriers including weak connectivity and lack of national identity documents (CIF, n.d.; World Bank, 2024b). A universal price benefit, albeit a regressive one, has therefore been replaced by targeted compensation covering only part of the exposed population.

The consequences became clear in July 2025. After diesel rose from Kz300 to Kz400 per litre, minibus taxi associations launched a strike that escalated into unrest across several provinces. The presidency reported 22 deaths, 197 injuries, and more than 1,200 arrests (Reuters, 2025). The government subsequently postponed full subsidy elimination from 2025 to 2028 (IMF, 2025). The verdict is therefore precise: The reform is fiscally effective but politically and socially fragile, and that fragility now determines its timetable.

Fiscal pressure helps explain why compensation risks becoming an afterthought. External debt service was projected at US$10.5 billion in 2025 (IMF, 2025), while 39% of Angola’s debt servicing in 2023 was guaranteed by oil (Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative [EITI], n.d.). This does not demonstrate that subsidy savings flowed directly to creditors. It does, however, reveal the risk: Without transparent earmarking, debt service can take priority while compensation remains a residual expenditure.

One Improvement: An Automatic Compensation Trigger

By 2030, Angola’s National Assembly should enact an automatic compensation trigger. No further fuel price increase should take effect until the Ministry of Finance has transferred the modelled cost of compensation into an audited social compensation account, subject to a ceiling of 30% of expected savings, and an independent audit has verified that affected households can receive an inflation-adjusted Kwenda payment before the increase. The 30% ceiling reflects World Bank modelling of the upper-bound cost of offsetting price effects in transport and fisheries (Bambe et al., 2024). Assisted and offline registration, using alternative identity verification where necessary, should be an implementation condition rather than a separate programme.

The trigger would be financed from the reform’s gross savings while preserving at least 70% for the wider budget. It is realistic because it requires a budget rule, not a new transfer system; uses Kwenda’s existing payment infrastructure; and fits Angola’s revised 2028 timetable. Angola has begun dismantling a welfare state disguised as a fuel price. An automatic trigger would rebuild social protection in plain sight, ensuring that every future price increase arrives with proof that citizens, not only creditors, receive a share of the dividend.

About the author

Kaashvi Sheoran is a graduate student working in policy research based in the Netherlands. She graduated cum laude from Amsterdam University College, specialising in international relations and political economy, with a minor in Conflict Studies and Sustainability. Her research explores how economic and institutional arrangements shape development, vulnerability and public policy outcomes. She has undertaken policy research at NITI Aayog, Government of India, and contributed to a Clingendael-linked research project through the Netherlands–Asia Honours Summer School. She is particularly interested in translating rigorous research into practical and socially equitable policy recommendations.
